At CRYPTO 2019, Gohr showed the signiﬁcant advantages of neural distinguishers over traditional distinguishers in differential
cryptanalysis. At fast software encryption (FSE) 2024, Bellini et al. provided a generic tool to automatically train the (related-key)
differential neural distinguishers for different block ciphers. In this paper, based on the intrinsic principle of differential cryptanalysis and neural distinguisher, we propose a superior (related-key) differential neural distinguisher that uses the ciphertext pairs
generated by two different differences. In addition, we give a framework to automatically train our (related-key) differential neural
distinguisher with four steps: difference selection, sample generation, training pipeline, and evaluation scheme. To demonstrate the
effectiveness of our approach, we apply it to the block ciphers: Simon, Speck, Simeck, and Hight. Compared to the existing results,
our method can provide improved accuracy and even increase the number of rounds that can be analyzed. The source codes are
available in https://github.com/differentialdistinguisher/AutoND_New.

1. Introduction
Differential cryptanalysis [1] is one of the most effective techniques to evaluate the security of block ciphers. It distinguishes
the block cipher from random permutations by analyzing the
propagation properties of plaintext differences. In traditional
differential cryptanalysis, cryptanalysts usually choose the differential path with the highest probability to perform this task.
To search for the differential path with the highest probability,
the most widely used methods are the branch-and-bound [2],
Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) [3], Constraint
Programming (CP) [4], and Boolean Satisﬁability problems
or Satisﬁability modulo theories (SAT/SMT) [5].
In recent years, with the continuous development of deep
learning, cryptologists have begun to explore the neural network aided differential cryptanalysis technology. At Crypto
2019, Gohr [6] ﬁrst introduced the concept of differential
neural distinguisher and showed its superior performance
over the traditional differential distinguisher for Speck with

block size 32 bits and key length 64 bits (Speck32/64) [7].
With the help of neural distinguishers in rounds 6, 7, and 8,
he successfully reduced the computational complexity of
11th and 12th key recovery attacks against Speck32/64. Since
then, this innovative approach has been applied to different
block ciphers, such as Simon [7], Present [8] and GIMLI [9],
and so on. In neural network aided differential cryptanalysis,
the core task is to construct a neural distinguisher with high
accuracy. Since the accuracy of a neural distinguisher
represents its ability to discern the block cipher from the
random permutation. In order to enhance the accuracy of
neural distinguishers, cryptanalysts mainly focus on two
aspects: neural network architecture and input data format.
In terms of neural network architecture, Bao et al. [10]
compared three different network structures for Simon with
block size 32 bits and key length 64 bits (Simon32/64) [7],
including Residual Network (ResNet) [11], Dense Network
(DenseNet) [12] and Squeeze-and-Excitation Network (SENet)
[13]. Inspired by the structure of GoogLeNet [14], Zhang,

IET Information Security

Wang, and Wang [15] and Zhang, Wang, and Chen [16] used
multiple parallel convolutional layers to capture more dimensional information. Subsequently, Gohr, Leander, and Neumann
[17] presented the effects of various hyperparameters on the
accuracy of the neural distinguishers. In order to provide a
generic training pipeline for differential cryptanalysis, Bellini et al.
[18] used dilated convolution [19] to address the problem of
near and long-range dependencies. In addition, they introduced
an evolutionary algorithm to search for the candidate differences
with high bias scores for constructing neural distinguishers.
These two components together form an automated framework
for training differential neural distinguishers.
In terms of input data format, Baksi [20] adopted the
output differences of the t (≥ 2) input differences to train the
differential neural distinguisher by simulating the all-in-one
method in [21]. At Eurocrypt 2021, Benamira et al. [22]
improved the accuracy of neural distinguishers by using
batches of ciphertext inputs. Subsequently, Chen et al. [23]
adopted multiple ciphertext pairs to effectively improve the
accuracy of the neural distinguisher for Speck32/64 [7], Chaskey [24], Present64/80 [8], data encryption standard (DES)
[25] and Secure Hash Algorithm 3-256 (SHA3-256) [26]. In
addition, there are some studies to improve the accuracy of
the neural distinguisher by providing the derivation information from the previous rounds, such as [10, 15, 16, 27].
So far, most studies of neural distinguishers have followed
Gohr’s [6]framework, which considers only a single input difference. In contrast, there are few studies considering multiple
input differences, only [20, 28, 29]. However, it is worth noting
that this approach has the potential to yield superior neural
distinguishers. In this paper, we aim to study this further.
1.1. Our Contributions. First, we discuss the advantages of
using two different differences to construct the neural distinguishers compared to using a single difference or more than
two differences. Then, we provide an automated framework
to build the neural distinguishers using two differences with
four steps: difference selection, sample generation, training
pipeline, and evaluation scheme. To validate the effectiveness
of our approach, we apply it to four block ciphers: Simon,
Speck, Simeck, and Hight, and achieve improved accuracy
under the same conditions. Using the simple training pipeline, we can achieve the following enhancements compared
with the results achieved by Bellini et al. [18].
• For Simon32/64 and Simon with block size 48 bits and
key length 96 bits (Simon48/96), we improve the accuracy
of their 11-round and 12-round neural distinguishers
from 0.516 and 0.506 to 0.525 and 0.515, respectively.
For Simon with block size 64 bits and key length 128
bits (Simon64/128), we not only obtain a superior 13round neural distinguisher, but also achieve a 14-round
neural distinguisher with the accuracy of 0.506.
• For Speck32/64 and Speck with block size 64 bits and
key length 128 bits (Speck64/128), we enhance the
accuracy of their 8-round neural distinguishers from
0.508 and 0.532 to 0.514 and 0.559, respectively. For
Speck with block size 48 bits and key length 96 bits

(Speck48/96), we not only obtain a better 7-round
neural distinguisher, but also an 8-round neural distinguisher with accuracy of 0.506.
• For Simeck with block size 32 bits and key length 64 bits
(Simeck32/64), we increase the accuracy of its 11-round
neural distinguisher from 0.516 to 0.525. For Simeck with
block size 48 bits and key length 96 bits (Simeck48/96)
and Simon64/128, we not only obtain improved 14-round
or 17-round neural distinguishers, but also achieve the
better neural distinguishers in the rounds 15 and 18.
• For Hight, we raise the accuracy of its 14-round relatedkey differential neural distinguisher from 0.563 to 0.640.
By employing the polishing step, we can further enhance
the accuracy of our neural distinguishers. However, this process requires a lot of computational resources and time.
Therefore, we only apply it to Simon32/64, Simeck32/64,
and Speck32/64. For Simon32/64 and Simeck32/64, we not
only achieve a neural distinguisher with higher accuracy for
rounds 11, but also obtain a 12-round neural distinguisher
with accuracy of 0.505. Even compared to the neural distinguishers using more complex data samples in [10, 15], our
neural distinguishers is competitive. For Speck32/64, we
achieve a neural distinguisher with an accuracy of 0.519,
which is better than the accuracy of 0.514 achieved by existing neural distinguishers. Our neural distinguishers are also
comparable when converted to the neural distinguishers
using multiple ciphertext pairs, as shown in Table 1. All these
results show the effectiveness of our method in improving
the accuracy of neural distinguishers.
1.2. Organization. Section 2 provides some concepts and
principles of (related-key) differential cryptanalysis and neural distinguishers. Section 3 details the automated construction process of our (related-key) differential neural distinguisher
using two differences. The enhanced (related-key) differential
neural distinguishers are constructed for Simon, Speck, Simeck,
and Hight in Section 4. Section 5 concludes this paper.

2.2. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). CNN is a feedforward neural network with convolutional architecture. It is
widely used in various ﬁelds, such as image recognition [32, 33],
video analysis [34, 35], natural language processing [36, 37], and
so on. A CNN usually comprises convolutional layers, pooling
layers, and fully connected layers. Among them, the convolutional layer is used to extract features through convolution operation. The pooling layer performs down-sampling to reduce the
parameters and computation of the neural network. The fully
connected layer integrates the outputs of the convolutional and
pooling layers for the ﬁnal classiﬁcation and prediction.
In this paper, we use not only standard convolutions but
also dilated convolutions [19]. It introduces a parameter
called dilation rate to deﬁne the spacing between the elements of the convolution kernel. By adjusting dilation rate,
we can expand the receptive ﬁeld of the kernel while maintaining a constant number of parameters. This approach can
capture long-range dependencies in the data more effectively.

2.3. (Related-Key) Differential Neural Distinguisher. At
CRYPTO 2019, Gohr [6] ﬁrst introduced the real and random
approach for training the neural distinguishers with ciphertext
pairs. This is an important milestone in the application of deep
learning techniques to differential cryptanalysis. Later, the
differential neural distinguisher was extended to the relatedkey scenario by Wang, Wang, and He [28]. Since then, the
(related-key) neural distinguisher has been widely used in
different block ciphers, such as Simon [10, 18], Simeck [31,
38], Present [16, 18, 39], and others.
2.3.1. Sample Composition. In [6], Gohr’s differential neural
distinguishers were trained using two types of ciphertext
pairs. One type of ciphertext pairs, labeled with 1, is obtained
by encrypting the plaintext pairs with the difference α using
the same master key. The other class of ciphertext pairs,
labeled with 0, is obtained by encrypting the random plaintext pairs using the same master key. In the related-key scenario, these two types of ciphertext pairs are obtained by
encrypting the above plaintext pairs using the master key
pairs that satisfy the difference δ.
2.3.2. Difference Evaluation. At fast software encryption
(FSE) 2024, Bellini et al. [18] showed that the weighted
bias score of ciphertext differences can approximately represent the suitability of different differences for constructing
(related-key) differential neural distinguishers. However,
computing the exact weighted bias score is impractical
because it requires enumerating all keys and plaintexts, but
we can approximate it using t samples. The related deﬁnitions are as follows:

2.3.3. Training Process. The complete training process of a
(related-key) differential neural distinguisher consists of two
parts: an online phase and an ofﬂine phase. The online phase
focuses on training the (related-key) differential neural distinguisher, while the ofﬂine phase uses the trained (relatedkey) differential neural distinguisher to execute the attack. In
the online phase, the plaintext difference and the key difference used to generate the samples should be selected ﬁrst. For
the single-key scenario, the key difference is 0. In this paper,
we use the weighted bias scores of ciphertext difference to
approximately evaluate different differences. Once the difference is chosen, we proceed to generate the training set and
test set. Each set comprises half of the positive samples
labeled as 1 and half of the negative samples labeled as 0.
In [6, 18], the positive sample is the ciphertext pairs generated by the selected difference, while the negative sample is
the ciphertext pairs generated by random difference. Subsequently, the training set is used to train the neural network
model. If the accuracy of this model surpasses 0.5, it indicates
that we successfully obtain a usable (related-key) differential
neural distinguisher.
In the ofﬂine phase, the pretrained neural network model
is used to distinguish the ciphertext pairs generated by the
block cipher or random permutation. If over half of the
ciphertext pairs score above 0.5, we deduce that they stem
from the block cipher; otherwise, they originate from the
random permutation.

Neural Distinguishers
In this section, we ﬁrst introduce the motivation to construct
our neural distinguisher using two differences instead of just
one or more. Subsequently, we give an automated process
for constructing our neural distinguisher with four steps:
difference selection, sample generation, training pipeline,
and evaluation scheme.
3.1. Motivation. At Eurocrypt 2021, Benamira et al. [22]
showed that Gohr’s neural distinguisher in [6] can well identify ciphertext pairs that exhibit truncated differences in the
last two rounds for Speck32/64, suggesting that it may acquire
differential–linear characteristics. Subsequently, Gohr, Leander,
and Neumann [17] further investigated the generality of this
conclusion in various ciphers. Their study showed that the neural distinguisher can indeed learn features beyond the differences
from the ciphertext pairs for Katan [40] and ChaCha [41].

However, for Simon [7], Present [8], and Skinny [42], only the
differences in ciphertext pairs provide useful information. In
addition, their study highlights the close connection between
the accuracy of the neural distinguisher and the mean absolute
distance between the ciphertext difference distribution and the
uniform distribution.
Based on these studies, we choose to build the differential
neural distinguisher using the ciphertext pairs generated by
two different nonzero input differences. The reason for choosing
two differences, rather than one or more, is mainly due to the
consideration of how the input differences propagate and how
the output differences conﬂict. If only one input difference is
selected, as the number of rounds increases, some output differences will tend to be uniformly distributed due to the confusion
and diffusion properties of the block cipher. This poses a challenge for the neural network to distinguish them from uniformly
distributed ciphertext pairs. However, if these uniformly distributed ciphertext pairs are generated by another, more favorable,
input difference, the average absolute distance between their
output differences will be more pronounced. This enhancement
would allow the neural network to distinguish them more effectively, since both of them follow their own unique distributions.
There are two reasons for limiting the number of input
differences to two rather than more: ﬁrst, the input differences that retain their own unique distributions after several
rounds are rare, and second, the more ciphertext pairs there
are, the higher the possibility of collision.
3.2. Difference Selection. The scheme of Bellini et al. [18]. At
FSE 2024, Bellini et al. [18] showed that the weighted bias
score can approximately evaluate the suitability of different
differences for constructing (related-key) differential neural
distinguishers, as outlined in Section 2.3. Additionally, they

also propose a genetic algorithm to automatically generate
suitable differences, as detailed in Algorithm 1. In this algorithm, the initial population consists of 1024 random input
differences. They are then sorted in descending order according to their bias score e
b tr ð⋅Þ. The 32 best differences form the
new population. Subsequently, there are 50 population iterations, each of which continuously generates new candidate
differences by performing bit-wise XOR on two differences
in the current population and ﬂipping a random bit with
probability 10%. Among these candidates, the 32 candidate
differences with the highest bias scores go to the next
iteration.
In order to enhance the algorithm’s generality and prevent instances where certain differences exhibit low bias
scores in the early rounds but high bias scores in later
rounds, this genetic algorithm starts at round 1 and increases
until the highest bias score is less than the threshold Tb of
0:05. We denote the maximum number of rounds at this time
as R. The R-round weighted bias score is used as the ﬁnal
evaluation metric for all candidate differences. The differences
with scores within 10% of the optimal bias score are taken as
the ﬁnal candidate differences for constructing the (relatedkey) differential neural distinguishers.

3.2.1. Our Scheme. However, our neural distinguisher requires
two differences, so the approach outlined in [18] does not
meet our requirements. Our analysis in Section 3.1 shows that
these two differences should satisfy two basic conditions.
First, they must exhibit favorable weighted bias scores to
ensure that their ciphertext pairs contain unique and easily
identiﬁable features. Second, maximize the difference between
their ciphertext pairs as much as possible, thereby, enhancing
the ability of the neural network to distinguish them.

3.4.1. DBitNet. An overview of the DBitNet is shown in
Figure 1. Its input is the binary representations of ciphertext
pairs with the shape ðinput size; 1Þ, where input size is
twice the length of the ciphertext. The input data is then
normalized to the range ½ −1; 1. The normalized output
passes through one Module1, two Module2, one Module3,
and then reaches the output layer.
Module1 integrates dilated convolution, batch normalization (BN), rectiﬁed linear unit (ReLU) activation functions, and
skip connections through addition operations (ADD). In the
ﬁrst convolution layer, the dilation rate Dri is dynamically
computed by repeatedly dividing the input size by 2 and subtracting 1 until it reaches a minimum value of 3, that is, Dri ¼
input size ÷ ði × 2Þ − 1, where i starts from 0 and increases
until Dri equals 3. As the dilation rate Dri is continuously
halved, the number of output ﬁlters Nf i is gradually increased
by 16 from the initial 32, i.e., Nf i ¼ 32 þ i × 16. This can capture varying degrees of long-range dependence. In the second
convolution layer, the dilation rate is ﬁxed to 1 to explore the
dependencies between neighboring bits. Module2 contain a
dense layer with d1 ¼ 256 neurons, a BN, and a ReLU activation function. Module3 has the same architecture as Module2
but contains d2 ¼ 64 neurons. The output layer is an output
unit with the sigmoid activation function.

3.4.2. The Simple Training Pipeline. In this paper, we reuse
the simple pipeline proposed by Bellini et al. in [18] to automate the training of our (related-key) neural distinguishers

with the maximum number of rounds instead of the manual
staged training strategy in [6, 10, 15]. This simple training
pipeline ﬁrst trains a neural distinguisher directly from the
ﬁrst two rounds with weighted bias scores greater than Tb ,
and then, reuses the distinguisher from the previous round to
train the distinguisher for the next round until the accuracy
of the neural distinguisher is less than a threshold of 0.505. In
addition, the Amsgrad algorithm [43] introduced at International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) 2018
is adopted to automatically adjust the learning rate to avoid
manual work.
3.4.3. A Polishing Step. In most cases, the simple training
pipeline is enough to generate competitive distinguishers
with the highest number of round compared to the staged
training strategy used in [6, 10, 15]. However, it is worth
noting that the staged training strategy retrains the neural
network with 109 training samples, while the simple training
pipeline only uses 107 . As a result, the neural distinguisher
obtained from the simple training pipeline may not outperform the neural distinguisher trained with the staged training
strategy in the accuracy of the highest number of rounds. To
solve this problem, a polishing step is introduced to further
improve the accuracy of the neural distinguisher by utilizing
109 training samples. The neural distinguisher with the maximum number of rounds is retrained three times by using the
Adam optimizer with learning rates of 10−4 , 10−5 , and 10−6 ,
respectively.

cipher with 64-bit block size and 128-bit key length. It

its key scheme also contains the whitened key besides the
round key. In addition, there is an initial transformation and
a ﬁnal transformation before and after the round function.
Unlike most block ciphers, its encryption scheme also requires
a whitening key in addition to a round key for the initial transformation and the ﬁnal transformation before and after the
round function.

5. Conclusion and Discussion
In this paper, we ﬁrst discuss the limitation of neural distinguisher using only a single difference, and point out the
advantages of constructing a neural distinguisher using two
differences from the inherent principles of neural distinguishers and differential cryptanalysis. We then provide a
generic training pipeline for our neural distinguishers, which

9
consists of four steps: difference selection, sample generation,
training pipeline, and evaluation scheme. Finally, to demonstrate the effectiveness of our method, we apply it to four
different block ciphers: Simon, Speck, Simeck, and Hight,
and obtain improved accuracy over existing results.
For the single-key scenario, the framework of Bellini et al.
[18] that we used can automatically construct neural distinguishers. But for the related-key differential neural distinguisher, we need to manually ﬁlter the plaintext difference
and key difference, since Algorithm 1 cannot automatically
ﬁnd the satisfactory results in the huge combination space of
plaintext difference and key difference. We will further investigate this problem in the future.
