# A New (Related‐Key) Neural Distinguisher Using Two Differences for Differential Cryptanalysis

**Authors:** Gao Wang, Gaoli Wang, Siwei Sun

**Source PDF:** `2024_two_difference_related_key_nd.pdf`

## Abstract

At CRYPTO 2019, Gohr showed the significant advantages of neural distinguishers over traditional distinguishers in differential cryptanalysis. At fast software encryption (FSE) 2024, Bellini et al. provided a generic tool to automatically train the (related-key) differential neural distinguishers for different block ciphers. In this paper, based on the intrinsic principle of differential cryptanalysis and neural distinguisher, we propose a superior (related-key) differential neural distinguisher that uses the ciphertext pairs generated by two different differences. In addition, we give a framework to automatically train our (related-key) differential neural distinguisher with four steps: difference selection, sample generation, training pipeline, and evaluation scheme. To demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, we apply it to the block ciphers: Simon, Speck, Simeck, and Hight. Compared to the existing results, our method can provide improved accuracy and even increase the number of rounds that can be analyzed. The source codes are available in https://github.com/differentialdistinguisher/AutoND_New .

## Introduction

Differential cryptanalysis [1] is one of the most effective techniques to evaluate the security of block ciphers. It distinguishes the block cipher from random permutations by analyzing the propagation properties of plaintext differences. In traditional differential cryptanalysis, cryptanalysts usually choose the differential path with the highest probability to perform this task. To search for the differential path with the highest probability, the most widely used methods are the branch-and-bound [2] , Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) [3] , Constraint Programming (CP) [4] , and Boolean Satisfiability problems or Satisfiability modulo theories (SAT/SMT) [5] .

In recent years, with the continuous development of deep learning, cryptologists have begun to explore the neural network aided differential cryptanalysis technology. At Crypto 2019, Gohr [6] first introduced the concept of differential neural distinguisher and showed its superior performance over the traditional differential distinguisher for Speck with block size 32 bits and key length 64 bits (Speck32/64) [7] . With the help of neural distinguishers in rounds 6, 7, and 8, he successfully reduced the computational complexity of 11th and 12th key recovery attacks against Speck32/64. Since then, this innovative approach has been applied to different block ciphers, such as Simon [7] , Present [8] and GIMLI [9] , and so on. In neural network aided differential cryptanalysis, the core task is to construct a neural distinguisher with high accuracy. Since the accuracy of a neural distinguisher represents its ability to discern the block cipher from the random permutation. In order to enhance the accuracy of neural distinguishers, cryptanalysts mainly focus on two aspects: neural network architecture and input data format.

In terms of neural network architecture, Bao et al. [10] compared three different network structures for Simon with block size 32 bits and key length 64 bits (Simon32/64) [7] , including Residual Network (ResNet) [11] , Dense Network (DenseNet) [12] and Squeeze-and-Excitation Network (SENet) [13] . Inspired by the structure of GoogLeNet [14] , Zhang, Wang, and Wang [15] and Zhang, Wang, and Chen [16] used multiple parallel convolutional layers to capture more dimensional information. Subsequently, Gohr, Leander, and Neumann [17] presented the effects of various hyperparameters on the accuracy of the neural distinguishers. In order to provide a generic training pipeline for differential cryptanalysis, Bellini et al. [18] used dilated convolution [19] to address the problem of near and long-range dependencies. In addition, they introduced an evolutionary algorithm to search for the candidate differences with high bias scores for constructing neural distinguishers. These two components together form an automated framework for training differential neural distinguishers.

In terms of input data format, Baksi [20] adopted the output differences of the t (≥ 2) input differences to train the differential neural distinguisher by simulating the all-in-one method in [21] . At Eurocrypt 2021, Benamira et al. [22] improved the accuracy of neural distinguishers by using batches of ciphertext inputs. Subsequently, Chen et al. [23] adopted multiple ciphertext pairs to effectively improve the accuracy of the neural distinguisher for Speck32/64 [7] , Chaskey [24] , Present64/80 [8] , data encryption standard (DES) [25] and Secure Hash Algorithm 3-256 (SHA3-256) [26] . In addition, there are some studies to improve the accuracy of the neural distinguisher by providing the derivation information from the previous rounds, such as [10, 15, 16, 27] .

So far, most studies of neural distinguishers have followed Gohr's [6] framework, which considers only a single input difference. In contrast, there are few studies considering multiple input differences, only [20, 28, 29] . However, it is worth noting that this approach has the potential to yield superior neural distinguishers. In this paper, we aim to study this further.


## Our Contributions.

First, we discuss the advantages of using two different differences to construct the neural distinguishers compared to using a single difference or more than two differences. To validate the effectiveness of our approach, we apply it to four block ciphers: Simon, Speck, Simeck, and Hight, and achieve improved accuracy under the same conditions. For Simon32/64 and Simeck32/64, we not only achieve a neural distinguisher with higher accuracy for rounds 11, but also obtain a 12-round neural distinguisher with accuracy of 0.505. Even compared to the neural distinguishers using more complex data samples in [10, 15] , our neural distinguishers is competitive. For Speck32/64, we achieve a neural distinguisher with an accuracy of 0.519, which is better than the accuracy of 0.514 achieved by existing neural distinguishers. Our neural distinguishers are also comparable when converted to the neural distinguishers using multiple ciphertext pairs, as shown in Table 1 . All these results show the effectiveness of our method in improving the accuracy of neural distinguishers.

## Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).

CNN is a feedforward neural network with convolutional architecture. It is widely used in various fields, such as image recognition [32, 33] , video analysis [34, 35] , natural language processing [36, 37] , and so on. A CNN usually comprises convolutional layers, pooling layers, and fully connected layers. Among them, the convolutional layer is used to extract features through convolution operation. The pooling layer performs down-sampling to reduce the parameters and computation of the neural network. The fully connected layer integrates the outputs of the convolutional and pooling layers for the final classification and prediction.

In this paper, we use not only standard convolutions but also dilated convolutions [19] . It introduces a parameter called dilation rate to define the spacing between the elements of the convolution kernel. By adjusting dilation rate, we can expand the receptive field of the kernel while maintaining a constant number of parameters. This approach can capture long-range dependencies in the data more effectively.


## (Related-Key) Differential Neural Distinguisher.

At CRYPTO 2019, Gohr [6] first introduced the real and random approach for training the neural distinguishers with ciphertext pairs. This is an important milestone in the application of deep learning techniques to differential cryptanalysis. Later, the differential neural distinguisher was extended to the relatedkey scenario by Wang, Wang, and He [28] . Since then, the (related-key) neural distinguisher has been widely used in different block ciphers, such as Simon [10, 18] , Simeck [31, 38] , Present [16, 18, 39] , and others.


## Sample Composition.

In [6] , Gohr's differential neural distinguishers were trained using two types of ciphertext pairs. One type of ciphertext pairs, labeled with 1, is obtained by encrypting the plaintext pairs with the difference α using the same master key. The other class of ciphertext pairs, labeled with 0, is obtained by encrypting the random plaintext pairs using the same master key. In the related-key scenario, these two types of ciphertext pairs are obtained by encrypting the above plaintext pairs using the master key pairs that satisfy the difference δ.

## Training Process.

The complete training process of a (related-key) differential neural distinguisher consists of two parts: an online phase and an offline phase. The online phase focuses on training the (related-key) differential neural distinguisher, while the offline phase uses the trained (relatedkey) differential neural distinguisher to execute the attack. In the online phase, the plaintext difference and the key difference used to generate the samples should be selected first. For the single-key scenario, the key difference is 0. In this paper, we use the weighted bias scores of ciphertext difference to approximately evaluate different differences. Once the difference is chosen, we proceed to generate the training set and test set. Each set comprises half of the positive samples labeled as 1 and half of the negative samples labeled as 0.

In [6, 18] , the positive sample is the ciphertext pairs generated by the selected difference, while the negative sample is the ciphertext pairs generated by random difference. Subsequently, the training set is used to train the neural network model. If the accuracy of this model surpasses 0.5, it indicates that we successfully obtain a usable (related-key) differential neural distinguisher.

In the offline phase, the pretrained neural network model is used to distinguish the ciphertext pairs generated by the block cipher or random permutation. If over half of the ciphertext pairs score above 0.5, we deduce that they stem from the block cipher; otherwise, they originate from the random permutation.


## Automated Construction of Our Neural Distinguishers

In this section, we first introduce the motivation to construct our neural distinguisher using two differences instead of just one or more. Subsequently, we give an automated process for constructing our neural distinguisher with four steps: difference selection, sample generation, training pipeline, and evaluation scheme.


## Motivation.

At Eurocrypt 2021, Benamira et al. [22] showed that Gohr's neural distinguisher in [6] can well identify ciphertext pairs that exhibit truncated differences in the last two rounds for Speck32/64, suggesting that it may acquire differential-linear characteristics. Subsequently, Gohr, Leander, and Neumann [17] further investigated the generality of this conclusion in various ciphers. Their study showed that the neural distinguisher can indeed learn features beyond the differences from the ciphertext pairs for Katan [40] and ChaCha [41] .

However, for Simon [7] , Present [8] , and Skinny [42] , only the differences in ciphertext pairs provide useful information. In addition, their study highlights the close connection between the accuracy of the neural distinguisher and the mean absolute distance between the ciphertext difference distribution and the uniform distribution.

Based on these studies, we choose to build the differential neural distinguisher using the ciphertext pairs generated by two different nonzero input differences. The reason for choosing two differences, rather than one or more, is mainly due to the consideration of how the input differences propagate and how the output differences conflict. If only one input difference is selected, as the number of rounds increases, some output differences will tend to be uniformly distributed due to the confusion and diffusion properties of the block cipher. This poses a challenge for the neural network to distinguish them from uniformly distributed ciphertext pairs. However, if these uniformly distributed ciphertext pairs are generated by another, more favorable, input difference, the average absolute distance between their output differences will be more pronounced. This enhancement would allow the neural network to distinguish them more effectively, since both of them follow their own unique distributions.

There are two reasons for limiting the number of input differences to two rather than more: first, the input differences that retain their own unique distributions after several rounds are rare, and second, the more ciphertext pairs there are, the higher the possibility of collision.


## Difference Selection.

The scheme of Bellini et al. [18] . At FSE 2024, Bellini et al. [18] showed that the weighted bias score can approximately evaluate the suitability of different differences for constructing (related-key) differential neural distinguishers, as outlined in Section 2.3. Additionally, they also propose a genetic algorithm to automatically generate suitable differences, as detailed in Algorithm 1. In this algorithm, the initial population consists of 1024 random input differences. They are then sorted in descending order according to their bias score e b t r ð⋅Þ: . The 32 best differences form the new population. Subsequently, there are 50 population iterations, each of which continuously generates new candidate differences by performing bit-wise XOR on two differences in the current population and flipping a random bit with probability 10%. Among these candidates, the 32 candidate differences with the highest bias scores go to the next iteration.

In order to enhance the algorithm's generality and prevent instances where certain differences exhibit low bias scores in the early rounds but high bias scores in later rounds, this genetic algorithm starts at round 1 and increases until the highest bias score is less than the threshold T b of 0:05. We denote the maximum number of rounds at this time as R. The R-round weighted bias score is used as the final evaluation metric for all candidate differences. The differences with scores within 10% of the optimal bias score are taken as the final candidate differences for constructing the (relatedkey) differential neural distinguishers.

## Training Pipeline.

The choice of neural network architecture and hyperparameters plays a pivotal role in training neural distinguishers. In the pioneering work, Gohr [6] adopted the ResNet [11] for Speck32/64. Subsequently, this architecture and its variants have been widely used, such as [10, 20, 38] . However, these architectures are tailored for specific block ciphers, since designing a generalized neural network for various block ciphers is a difficult task. To address this problem, Gohr, Leander, and Neumann [17] introduced an automatic hyperparameter tuning method and applied it to six different block ciphers. However, on the one hand, this method does not work for all ciphers, such as advanced encryption standard (AES), as shown in [18] , and on the other hand, it does not support neural distinguishers with the highest number of rounds, which would require a staged training approach. Therefore, Bellini et al. [18] used a generalized neural network called DBitNet to adapt different grouping ciphers. In addition, they provided a simple training pipeline to train the neural distinguisher with the maximum number of rounds and a polishing step to further enhance its accuracy by decreasing the learning rate of the Adam optimizer for three times, from 10 -4 to 10 -5 to 10 -6 .


## DBitNet.

An overview of the DBitNet is shown in Figure 1 . Its input is the binary representations of ciphertext pairs with the shape ðinput size; 1Þ: , where input size is twice the length of the ciphertext. The input data is then normalized to the range ½ -1; 1: . The normalized output passes through one Module1, two Module2, one Module3, and then reaches the output layer. Module1 integrates dilated convolution, batch normalization (BN), rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation functions, and skip connections through addition operations (ADD). In the first convolution layer, the dilation rate Dr i is dynamically computed by repeatedly dividing the input size by 2 and subtracting 1 until it reaches a minimum value of 3, that is, Dr i ¼ input size ÷ ði × 2Þ: -1, where i starts from 0 and increases until Dr i equals 3. As the dilation rate Dr i is continuously halved, the number of output filters Nf i is gradually increased by 16 from the initial 32, i.e., Nf i ¼ 32 þ i × 16. This can capture varying degrees of long-range dependence. In the second convolution layer, the dilation rate is fixed to 1 to explore the dependencies between neighboring bits. Module2 contain a dense layer with d 1 ¼ 256 neurons, a BN, and a ReLU activation function. Module3 has the same architecture as Module2 but contains d 2 ¼ 64 neurons. The output layer is an output unit with the sigmoid activation function.


## The Simple Training Pipeline.

In this paper, we reuse the simple pipeline proposed by Bellini et al. in [18] to automate the training of our (related-key) neural distinguishers with the maximum number of rounds instead of the manual staged training strategy in [6, 10, 15] . This simple training pipeline first trains a neural distinguisher directly from the first two rounds with weighted bias scores greater than T b , and then, reuses the distinguisher from the previous round to train the distinguisher for the next round until the accuracy of the neural distinguisher is less than a threshold of 0.505. In addition, the Amsgrad algorithm [43] introduced at International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) 2018 is adopted to automatically adjust the learning rate to avoid manual work.


## A Polishing Step.

In most cases, the simple training pipeline is enough to generate competitive distinguishers with the highest number of round compared to the staged training strategy used in [6, 10, 15] . However, it is worth noting that the staged training strategy retrains the neural network with 10 9 training samples, while the simple training pipeline only uses 10 7 . As a result, the neural distinguisher obtained from the simple training pipeline may not outperform the neural distinguisher trained with the staged training strategy in the accuracy of the highest number of rounds. To solve this problem, a polishing step is introduced to further improve the accuracy of the neural distinguisher by utilizing 10 9 training samples. The neural distinguisher with the maximum number of rounds is retrained three times by using the Adam optimizer with learning rates of 10 -4 , 10 -5 , and 10 -6 , respectively.

## Simon.

Simon [7] is the lightweight block cipher proposed by the National Security Agency (NSA) in 2013. ## Simeck.

Simeck is a lightweight block cipher proposed by Yang et al. [44] at International Conference on Cryptographic Hardware and Embedded Systems (CHES) 2015. It combines the design strengths of Simon and Speck. Its round function is very similar to Simon's, with the only difference being the choice of parameters for the circular shift. Its key expansion algorithm reuses its round function, similar to the block cipher Speck, and thus has superior performances in both software and hardware implementations.

For the Simeck cipher family [44] , we also consider its variants with block sizes of 32, 48, and 64 bits and key lengths of 64, 96, and 128 bits, that is, the Simeck32/64, Simeck48/96, and Simeck64/128, respectively. They have 16, 20, and 24 candidate differences satisfying ɛ ¼ 0:1. Their best neural distinguishers are constructed from 0x4000, 0x80000, and 0x2000000 with accuracies of 0.516, 0.515, and 0.514 in rounds 11, 14, and 17, respectively.

We then further construct our neural distinguishers through the simple training pipeline by fixing α 0 to 0x4000, 0x80000, and 0x2000000 and selecting α 1 from the remaining differences. For Simeck32/64, Simeck48/96, and Simeck64/128, the accuracies of our best neural distinguisher trained using α 1 ¼ 0x8000; 0x10000; and 0x20000 in rounds 11, 14, and 17 are 0.525, 0.520, and 0.519, respectively. In addition, we also obtain the neural distinguishers for 15round Simeck48/96 and 18-round Simeck64/128 using α 1 ¼ 0x2000 and 0x20000 with accuracies of 0.505 and 0.507, respectively. Using the polishing step, we can further improve the accuracy of our 11-round neural distinguisher to 0.527 for Simeck32/64. ## Hight.

Hight [45] is a generalized Feistel-like block cipher with 64-bit block size and 128-bit key length. It only relies on some basic operations, such as bit-wise XOR, addition modulo 2 8 , and left rotation. As a result, it can provide a lightweight hardware implementation with low cost and low power consumption. Unlike most block ciphers, its key scheme also contains the whitened key besides the round key. In addition, there is an initial transformation and a final transformation before and after the round function. Unlike most block ciphers, its encryption scheme also requires a whitening key in addition to a round key for the initial transformation and the final transformation before and after the round function.

Bellini et al. [18] yielded a 10-round single-key differential neural distinguisher and a 14-round related-key differential neural distinguisher for Hight. The number of rounds in the related-key scenario is significantly higher than that in the single-key scenario, so we focus on building the relatedkey differential neural distinguisher for Hight. The optimal 14-round related-key differential neural distinguisher is constructed with the plaintext difference 0x800000000000 and key difference 0x800000 with 40 epochs, so we set (0x800000000000, 0x800000) as α 0 and δ 0 to construct our related-key neural distinguisher using the same number of epochs.

Similar to the previous experiment, we first use Algorithm 1 to automatically generate the related-key candidate differences with high weighted bias scores, but the results are not satisfactory, which may be due to the large differential search space. The Algorithm 1 prefers the candidates with the plaintext difference of 0. Therefore, we choose α 1 and δ 1 from all the plaintext differences and key differences with hamming weight of 1. We first evaluate the weighted bias scores of their combinations and then choose the top 25 differences with the highest scores as the candidates for α 1 and δ 1 . Our best 14round neural distinguisher is constructed from the α 1 ¼ 0x40 and δ 1 ¼ 0x40000000000000000000 with an accuracy of 0.640, while it is 0.563 in [18] . ## Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we first discuss the limitation of neural distinguisher using only a single difference, and point out the advantages of constructing a neural distinguisher using two differences from the inherent principles of neural distinguishers and differential cryptanalysis. We then provide a generic training pipeline for our neural distinguishers, which consists of four steps: difference selection, sample generation, training pipeline, and evaluation scheme. Finally, to demonstrate the effectiveness of our method, we apply it to four different block ciphers: Simon, Speck, Simeck, and Hight, and obtain improved accuracy over existing results.

For the single-key scenario, the framework of Bellini et al. [18] that we used can automatically construct neural distinguishers. But for the related-key differential neural distinguisher, we need to manually filter the plaintext difference and key difference, since Algorithm 1 cannot automatically find the satisfactory results in the huge combination space of plaintext difference and key difference. We will further investigate this problem in the future.
